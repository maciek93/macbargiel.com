---
title: "Everything I own is a bet on the same thing"
date: 2025-06-08
tags: ["investing", "rationality"]
---

I put my accounts into one spreadsheet last month, which I had somehow never done. Not for any good reason, I'd just always looked at them separately, the pension here, the ISA there, the vesting schedule in a tab I open four times a year.

Seeing it in one place was not a nice experience.

My salary comes from a large tech company. A meaningful chunk of my compensation is that company's stock, arriving on a schedule I don't control. And then in the ISA, where I get to choose freely, where I could buy literally anything, I had chosen: more tech.

Not the same company, I'm not that daft. But the correlation between "large American tech company" and "other large American tech company" is high enough that the distinction is mostly cosmetic. When one of them has a bad year it is very rarely because of something specific to that one.

So it's one position. It has always been one position. I just had it spread across four tabs so it looked like four.

The thing that makes this worse than ordinary concentration is when the correlated bits go wrong together. If the sector turns properly, several things happen at once. The equity portion of my comp is worth less on paper. Refreshers get granted at a worse moment or get quietly smaller. Hiring slows, which changes what my job is and how secure it feels. And the ISA is down at the same time, for the same reason.

That's the actual risk. Not that any one of those happens, they're all survivable on their own, but that they're the same event wearing different hats. The moment I would most want savings to be steady is precisely the moment they wouldn't be.

Why did I do it? I've thought about this more than the situation probably deserves and I think it's two things, both of which I would have spotted instantly in someone else.

The first is that working in the industry feels like an edge. I read the internal stuff, I see what's being built, I have opinions about which products are good. It feels like information. But the market isn't pricing whether the product is good, it's pricing earnings against expectations that already have "the product is good" in them. I know a lot about a layer that the price is not about. That's not an edge, it's just familiarity, and familiarity is extremely convincing from the inside.

The second is more embarrassing, which is that the tech names are the ones I recognise. When you sit down to buy something, the names you know feel less risky than the names you don't, and that feeling has nothing to do with actual risk. It is just recognition. There's a well documented version of this where people over-invest in their own country's market for the same reason, and I would have said I was above it right up until I opened the spreadsheet.

What I've done about it is less than you'd think.

I didn't sell anything. Partly there are tax consequences to unpicking things at the wrong moment, partly it would mean acting decisively on a conclusion I'd reached about forty minutes earlier. So new money goes into broad index funds now, and the concentrated part gets diluted slowly by everything else growing around it. It'll take years. It's the lazy fix, and I picked it partly because it's lazy, since a plan I'll actually follow beats a better plan I'll abandon in March.

I also stopped counting the unvested equity as money. It's not money, it's a claim on a future where several things stay true. Writing it in the spreadsheet in a different colour was a small change that made the total look considerably less impressive, which I suspect is the honest number.

None of this is advice. I'm a person with a spreadsheet and an obvious blind spot I found late.

The part I keep turning over is that I'd have caught this in five seconds if it were someone else's portfolio. Someone describes their holdings to me, all in one sector, same sector they work in, and I'd say something. I looked at my own version of it for about six years without noticing, because it wasn't presented to me as a portfolio. It was presented as a job, and some benefits, and a hobby.
